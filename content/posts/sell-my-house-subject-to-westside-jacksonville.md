## What "Subject To" Means When You Sell a House in Westside Jacksonville

In a normal sale, the buyer gets a new loan (or pays cash), and your mortgage is paid off at closing.

In a **subject-to** sale, the buyer takes ownership of the house "subject to" your existing mortgage. Here's what that usually looks like:

- **The deed transfers to the buyer.** They own the house and take over the property.
- **The loan stays in your name.** Your mortgage isn't paid off, and it isn't formally assumed by the buyer.
- **The buyer makes the monthly payments** on your existing loan going forward.
- **Your lender is not part of the deal.** The lender doesn't approve it, and the loan terms don't change.

For a homeowner who owes close to what the house is worth, this can be a way to stop carrying the payment, avoid out-of-pocket selling costs, and move on, without waiting for the market to catch up.

## The Risks You Need to Understand

We want to be straight with you, because these risks are real and they fall mostly on the seller.

### The Due-on-Sale Clause

Most mortgages include a **due-on-sale clause**. It generally lets the lender demand the full loan balance if the property is transferred without the lender's consent. In a subject-to sale, the house *is* transferred, so the lender could call the loan due. Whether a lender ever does that varies, and nobody can promise you it won't happen. Ask your attorney to read your mortgage and explain what this means for you.

### The Loan Stays on Your Credit

Because the mortgage is still in your name, the payment history keeps showing up on your credit. If the buyer pays late or stops paying, it hurts *your* credit, not theirs. That's why who you sell to matters as much as the deal itself.

### Insurance, Taxes, and Escrow

The homeowner's insurance, property taxes, and any escrow account need to be handled correctly after the sale. These details should be spelled out in writing and reviewed by your attorney and title company.

## How to Protect Yourself in a Subject-To Sale

If you decide to explore this route, a few habits go a long way:

1. **Use a licensed title company or real estate attorney** to handle the closing and record the deed. Never do it on a handshake.
2. **Get everything in writing.** The contract should say who pays the mortgage, taxes, and insurance, and what happens if a payment is missed.
3. **Ask how you'll see payments being made.** Many sellers want ongoing access to the loan account or monthly proof that the payment cleared.
4. **Check out the buyer.** Ask how long they've been in business, look them up, and ask for references from title companies or attorneys they've worked with.
5. **Don't let anyone rush you.** A buyer who won't give you time to talk to an attorney is a buyer to walk away from.

## Subject-To vs. Other Ways to Sell

It helps to compare your options side by side before you pick one.

**Listing with an agent.** If you have enough equity to cover commissions and closing costs, a traditional sale pays off your loan completely and ends your connection to the house. If you don't have that equity, it can mean bringing cash to closing.

**A cash sale.** If there's some equity, a cash buyer can pay off your loan at closing, usually with no repairs and no showings. You can read more about [how we buy houses](/how-we-buy-houses/) for cash.

**A short sale.** If you owe more than the house is worth, your lender may agree to accept less than the balance. Short sales need lender approval and can take time.

**Subject-to.** Can work when there's little or no equity and the loan is current or close to it, but it comes with the risks above.

There's no single right answer. The best choice depends on your loan balance, your payment history, your timeline, and how much risk you're comfortable with.
