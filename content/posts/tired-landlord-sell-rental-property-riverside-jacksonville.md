## Signs You're Ready to Stop Being a Landlord

Every landlord has a bad month. But some signs point to something bigger than a rough patch:

- You dread phone calls because it's usually a repair or a problem.
- The rent barely covers the mortgage, taxes, and insurance, or doesn't cover them at all.
- Big-ticket items like the roof, HVAC, or plumbing are getting close to the end of their life.
- You've had more than one turnover, and each one costs you weeks of lost rent.
- You moved away from Jacksonville and managing from a distance is wearing you out.
- You'd rather have the money working somewhere else, or simply have peace of mind.

If a few of these sound familiar, it's fair to ask whether keeping the property still makes sense for you.

## Why Riverside Rentals Can Be Extra Work

Riverside is one of Jacksonville's older, most character-filled neighborhoods. Many of the homes and small apartment buildings here are decades old, with beautiful details and, often, aging systems behind the walls. Older houses can mean older wiring, older plumbing, and more frequent repairs.

Part of the area also sits inside the Riverside Avondale historic district. The City of Jacksonville has design guidelines for the district, and many exterior changes there go through the city's Certificate of Appropriateness process before work can start. That's part of what keeps the neighborhood special, but it can add steps and time to projects like replacing windows or making exterior changes. If your property is in the district, check with the City of Jacksonville's historic preservation staff before you plan any exterior work.

None of this makes Riverside a bad place to own. It just means some landlords here hit the "I'm done" point sooner than they expected.

## Your Options for Selling a Riverside Rental Property

There's no single right way to get out. Here are the paths most landlords consider.

### 1. Fix It Up and List It With an Agent

If the house is in good shape and empty, listing it can bring a strong price. The trade-off is time and upfront cost. You may need repairs, paint, cleaning, and staging. Buyers using loans usually need the house to pass an inspection and an appraisal. And while it's listed, you keep paying the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and utilities.

### 2. Sell With the Tenant in Place

You can generally sell a rental while a tenant is living in it. In most cases the lease stays in place and the new owner takes over as landlord. This can work well if your tenant pays on time, because some investors like buying a property that's already rented. It's harder if the tenant is behind or won't cooperate with showings. Your attorney can explain how the lease, notice rules, and security deposit should be handled.

### 3. Wait for the Lease to End

If your tenant's lease ends soon, you might wait, let the unit go vacant, and then sell. Just remember that a vacant house still costs money each month, and vacant homes can attract break-ins or damage if no one is checking on them.

## Things to Plan for Before You Sell a Rental

Selling a rental is different from selling the home you live in. Bring these questions to your tax advisor and attorney before you sign anything:

**Taxes on the sale.** Rental properties are treated differently from a primary home when it comes to taxes. Things like capital gains and depreciation you've taken over the years can affect what you owe. A tax professional can help you estimate this before you decide.

**A possible 1031 exchange.** Some investors look at a 1031 exchange to roll the proceeds into another investment property. There are strict rules and deadlines, so if this interests you, talk to a tax advisor and a qualified intermediary before the sale closes, not after.

**The security deposit and lease.** Florida has rules about how security deposits are handled, and they don't go away when you sell. Work out with your attorney how the deposit and any lease will transfer to the buyer.

**Your mortgage payoff.** Ask your lender for a payoff amount so you know what you'll walk away with.

**Utilities, insurance, and HOA (if any).** Line up when to cancel or transfer each one so nothing lapses or gets double-billed.
